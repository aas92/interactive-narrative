Meditation is older than medicine
Long before it reached the clinic, meditation was a spiritual practice. The word comes from the Latin meditārī, meaning to think something over1. Nearly every culture grew its own form: Dhikr in Islam, Lectio Divina in Christianity, Hitbodedut in Judaism, Vipassanā in Buddhism2, Prāṇāyāma in Hinduism, and Neidan in Daoism.

All of them begin with the same simple idea: turn your attention inward, on purpose. But the aim was faith, not health, and no one set out to measure them. Scroll to grow the tree from these roots, then climb to its modern crown.
Then science began to ask
In the late twentieth century, doctors took the oldest of these practices and began to measure it. The most studied form was mindfulness3, which grew out of Buddhist practice and became two clinical programs at the UMass Center for Mindfulness: MBSR (Mindfulness Based Stress Reduction) and MBCT (Mindfulness Based Cognitive Therapy). The core instruction sounds simple: notice your thoughts without judging them.

At the crown of the tree, circled in red, the question changes. It is no longer is this sacred? Now it is does it actually work, and how would we know?

To answer that, we looked at the evidence three ways: 2,411 clinical trials, heart rate readings taken during meditation sessions, and health surveys from 374 people.

What is meditation studied for?
Before we ask whether meditation works, it helps to see where the research has gone. We pooled 2,411 trials registered on ClinicalTrials.govB, combining mindfulness with other meditation traditions, and grouped each by the primary condition it set out to treat.

The effort is lopsided. Chronic pain, stress and burnout, and depression account for the largest share of trials, while anxiety and neurological or cognitive conditions are studied far less. The chart shows where meditation has been proposed as a treatment, not where it has been proven to work: a tall bar reflects research interest, not demonstrated benefit.

Whether that interest is justified is the question the rest of this piece takes up. A large multi-site randomized trial of self-administered mindfulness found a real reduction in stressF; from there we examine the underlying data on attentionA, heart rateC, sleepD, and the techniques themselvesE to move from how often meditation is studied to how well it actually works.
But volume isn't proof
Thousands of trials tell us meditation is studied, not that it works. To judge that, we have to look inside the body, beginning with the simplest question we can ask it: when you sit in stillness, does your heart actually slow down? The answer, it turns out, is more than one story.
What can a heartbeat tell us about meditation?

Before either of them begins, the two hearts are simply at rest, and already they look nothing alike. The chi practitioner settles near 81 beats a minute; the kundalini practitioner is noticeably calmer, around 61. Steady, even, unremarkable for now.

Then they begin, and the two hearts move in opposite directions. The chi practitioner’s slows, easing down by a few beats. The kundalini practitioner’s does the opposite, climbing upward past 90, driven by fast, forceful breathing. One word, “meditation,” pulling the body two completely different ways.

So meditation was never a single thing. The quiet, inward kind can gently settle your heart; the active, effortful kind revs it up. Neither is wrong; they simply serve different ends. And that is the real lesson: mindfulness works best as a complement to a healthy life, not a cure that stands on its own.

One caveat: this is a small study, just 8 chi and 4 kundalini practitioners, and each line here traces a single person. It shows the pattern cleanly, but treat the exact numbers as a signal, not a verdict.

Source: Peng et al., “Exaggerated Heart Rate Oscillations During Two Meditation Techniques,” Int. J. Cardiol. (1999). See Sources for full references.

So where does meditation clearly help?
Look back at that first chart for a moment. Of every condition people study meditation for, stress and burnout sat second from the top, behind only chronic pain. So stress was never a side topic here. It is one of the main reasons people reach for meditation at all.

And stress does not stay in your head. A large longitudinal study traces how chronic stress steadily wears down sleep, and how lost sleep then feeds back into more stress, a loop that taxes the heart, mood, and immune system along the way ( Zhang et al., 2024, The impact of stress on sleep quality). So the real question is not whether meditation can calm a single heartbeat. It is where stress fits in the larger shape of a person's life. Look at the loop below.

The life loop in one picture: meditation can't lower a heartbeat on command, but it can push on the one node that starts the cycle. The next charts test that link.

Stress and Sleep
This study followed 374 people, and the pattern is hard to miss. Stress and sleep quality are strongly, negatively linked (r = −0.90): the more stress someone carried, the worse they slept, and this held even for people with no insomnia or sleep apnea at all. That matters, because sleep is not a luxury. It is when the body repairs itself, so stress quietly stealing your sleep is stress quietly taxing your health. And it compounds. High stress lowers sleep quality, poor sleep leaves the body unable to recover, and a depleted body feels even more stress, tightening the loop on itself.
High stress, worse on every axis
This chart aggregates all the participants from the previously mentioned study and summarizes their findings: low-stress individuals average 8.3 sleep quality and 67 BPM resting heart rate; high-stress individuals fall to 5.9 and rise to 74 BPM.

Stress has a negative effect on sleep. If we want to sleep better, we have to find ways to lower our stress!
Meditation lowers reported stress after a single session
Alessandro Sparacio and several collaborators ran a 37 site study with over 2,200 participants to study how meditation affects how people report their levels of stress. The control group listened to a chapter from an audio book while the other groups practiced a guided meditation in one of three therapeutic meditation practices.

“All exercises proved to be more efficacious than the active control. We observed a mean difference of 0.27 (d = −0.56; 95% CI −0.43 to −0.69) between the control condition (M = 1.95, SD = 0.50) and the condition with the largest stress reduction (body scan: M = 1.68, SD = 0.46).
Our findings suggest that mindfulness may be beneficial for reducing self-reported short-term stress for English speakers from higher-income countries.” Self-Administered Mindfulness Interventions Reduce Stress in a Large, Randomized Controlled Multi-Site Study.

A complement, not a cure.
Mindfulness is not a magic fix, and the body's in-the-moment signals are too mixed to call it one. But it is a real, evidence-backed lever on the part of the chain you can actually move, your stress, and the sleep and health that follow from it. Used that way, alongside the rest of a healthy life rather than in place of it, the evidence says it earns its keep.
