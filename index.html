Meditation as a spiritual practice.
Across cultures, each religion has distinct contemplative practices: Dhikr in Islam, Lectio Divina in Christianity, Hitbodedut in Judaism, Vipassanā in the Pāli Buddhist tradition2, Prāṇāyāma in Hinduism, and Neidan in Daoism.

Meditation practices are diverse and widespread but there is a common root: quieting the mind and focusing attention. It wasn't until meditative practices spread throughout North America in the 20th century that Western Medicine became interested in studying its potential palliative benefits.
Meditation as an object of study.
Mindfulness became the most popular North American practice by the mid 20th century. It was adapted from the Theravāda Buddhist tradition and developed into two practices for stress relief and mental health.

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) are attributed to the work of Howard Kabat-Zinn, a medical researcher at the UMass Center for Mindfulness in the late 1970's.

The program is simple, but challenging: Observe your breath. Be present to your expereince without judging it.

Much research has been conducted on psychological and emotional conditions: Stress, Burnout and Depression. Studies on physiological diseases like Cardiovascular conditions, Neurological/Cognitive Disorders and Anxiety are much less numerous.
How Meditation affects our bodies
Meditation is about focusing on the breath. Our respiratory system delivers oxygen throughout the body in our blood. Science tells us that deep breathing regulates our nervous system and heart rate. We can use movement meditation to elevate our heart rate, like Yoga. Or we can use a different movement meditation, Chi Gong, to lower it. Both movement based practices target our respiration and heart rate, not in opposite directions.

The Holistic Health Picture

  1. Sleep

To maximize our health outcomes, we have to find ways to regularly meet all of these needs. How does meditation fit into these health pillars?
Stress and Sleep
Across 374 individuals, stress and sleep quality are strongly, negatively linked (r = −0.90). As reported stress increases, sleep quality decreases.

You can imagine how this moves in a cycle: The higher stress you feel, your sleep quality gets lower, your body can't recover in sleep, and the more stress you feel.
High stress, worse on every axis
Group people into low, mid, and high stress and the pattern holds across metrics: low-stress individuals average 8.3 sleep quality and 67 BPM resting heart rate; high-stress individuals fall to 5.9 and rise to 74 BPM. Each bar is scaled within its own range so the comparison is honest.
The honest conclusion
The data supports a measured claim, not a miracle. Meditation has strong, replicated benefits for attention and for the stress mechanisms that erode sleep — expert meditators show far less mind-wandering (0.32 vs 0.40 in novices). But within-session physiological effects like heart rate are inconsistent and technique-dependent. Meditation works best as one practice among several — paired with sleep hygiene, activity, and care — not as a standalone cure.
